# 08 — Workforce and Organisation

The pyramid's workforce is a solved problem in outline and an argued problem in detail. The evidence is unusually direct: the settlement, its cemetery, its garbage, its seals and its ration accounts all survive, and one of the best sources is a logbook written by the people doing the work.

## Herodotus and the 100,000

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE — more than two millennia after the fact — reported that Khufu compelled all Egyptians to work for him, in gangs of 100,000, each gang for three months, over twenty years [66][67].

Every element of that claim is now considered unreliable. He visited the pyramids roughly 2,700 years after they were built, and his figure was what he was told by local informants; his "100,000 men working in three shifts" is ambiguous between three gangs of 100,000 and 100,000 divided into three shifts [66][41]. The "slaves" framing comes from the same second-hand report. As NOVA put it to Mark Lehner: "Herodotus just claims he was told that" [66].

The number nevertheless persists, because it is the number that makes the story memorable. It should be retired.

## What the archaeological estimates actually say

| Estimate | Figure | Method | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Herodotus | 100,000, in gangs of 100,000 on three-month rotation | Second-hand, ~2,000 years later | [66][67] |
| Mendelssohn (1974) | 70,000 seasonal + up to 10,000 permanent masons | Physicist's estimate | [39] |
| Lehner (NOVA) | ~5,000 or fewer men for building, quarrying and hauling | Block-rate arithmetic: 340 stones/day | [66][40] |
| Tyldesley | ~4,000 primary labourers + 16,000–20,000 secondary | BBC synthesis | [41] |
| Digital Giza (Harvard) | 20,000–30,000 native Egyptians | Settlement and cemetery scale | [42] |
| Hawass | 36,000 permanent + temporary workmen | Settlement, tombs and cemetery | [40] |
| Houdin | ~4,000, using his internal-ramp method | Model-dependent | [50] |
| Energy-floor analysis | ~800-900 at the theoretical limit; ~3,300 with real overheads | Work-energy bound | [39] |

The spread is large because the estimates answer different questions. "4,000" counts people who quarry, haul and set stone. "36,000" counts everyone the state project touched, including the food chain and the administration. Both can be right at once, and the popular argument between them is largely a disagreement about what to count.

### Lehner's arithmetic, because it is the cleanest

Lehner's estimate does not rely on a settlement-size argument; it works forward from a required rate [66]:

- The Great Pyramid contains roughly 2.3 million blocks; over a 20-year build, the target is about **340 stones placed per day** [66].
- Even granting considerable leeway for tool efficiency, all 340 stones could be quarried in a day by something like **1,200 men**, working locally at Giza [66].
- Delivery requires 34 stones an hour in a ten-hour day; dividing by gangs of 20 men gives on the order of **2,000 men** [66].
- Adding the stone-setters, the total comes to **5,000 or fewer** for the core operation, within a 20–40 year window [66].

The 340-per-day figure is a reasonable, checkable number, and it is close to the independent rates quoted elsewhere: about 12 blocks placed per hour, day and night, equivalent to ~800 tonnes per day [6], and about one tonne placed every two to three minutes by human draw teams on a ramp of maximum 10% [44].

### The energy floor again

The work-energy bound from [Chapter 3](03-the-physics-of-hauling.md) gives a hard physical floor of roughly 800-900 men at perfect efficiency and about 3,300 with realistic overheads [39]. Any estimate of total pyramid employment that sits far below 3,300 is not describing the pyramid project; it is describing a subset of it.

## Seasonality and the rotation system

The labour force was not a permanent army of 100,000. It was a rotating levy.

The ancient Egyptian state used a **corvée** — a labour tax — and temple service was already organised into five *phyles* (work gangs), each subdivided into two divisions, working one month in ten [41]. Pyramid crews were almost certainly drawn from the same structure: a permanent salaried core of skilled technicians living in the pyramid village, and a much larger temporary population arriving for three- or four-month shifts during the flood season, camping in a less substantial camp alongside [41].

Lehner's own view is that people were rotated in and out of the raw labour force — a young man from a village in Middle Egypt might have seen a few hundred people in his village, perhaps at market day, and then found himself in a state labour gang. "It may not have been entirely coercion," he says, "it seems that everybody owed a labor tax" [66]. Hawass's version: permanent workmen were paid by the king and were the technicians who cut the stone, while others moved stone and came in rotation [66].

Herodotus's three-month rotation, in other words, may have been roughly right about the *interval* while being wildly wrong about the *headcount*.

## Heit el-Ghurab: what the settlement shows

The settlement excavated by AERA immediately southeast of the pyramids is the evidence base, and it is richer than a barracks. The site's own literature describes it as purpose-built by the crown to serve the construction of the pyramid complexes of Menkaure, Khafre and probably Khufu [25]. Its streets and alleyways were lined with craft workshops, industrial yards, bakeries, commissaries, kitchens, warehouses, small houses, and stately homes and offices for site administrators [25]. Massive royal silos stood in a large compound where grain was distributed to create the bread and beer used to feed the workforce [25].

Three findings from the site carry disproportionate weight.

**The galleries are barrack-like, and they were for rotating crews.** Lehner's team identified long gallery buildings and proposed they housed "a rotating labor force, perhaps as large as 1,600 to 2,000 workers" [67]. The galleries' relationship to the possible port at the site is discussed in [Chapter 2](02-logistics-and-waterways.md).

**There was medicine.** The faunal assemblage from the settlement includes animal bones showing signs of healed traumatic injuries, with evidence of some years of survival after the trauma [24]. This is the single most decisive fact against the "slave" framing. Slaves are not given years of convalescence and fed while they recover.

**The administration was documented.** Sealings recovered from the site include official sealings and far more numerous "informal" sealings impressed by everyday workers, bearing no epithet and no king's name, suggesting craftspeople working outside the formal administrative chain [68]. The bureaucracy was real, and it reached down to the level of individual work gangs.

## Diet and provisioning

The food record is quantitative, and it is the basis for estimating the workforce's size.

Richard Redding of AERA estimated that workers needed at least **45–50 g of protein per day** [69]. Roughly half could come from fish, beans, lentils and other non-meat sources; the other half from sheep, goat and cattle [69]. Combining that with the ratio of bones to meat yield, he determined that about **11 cattle and 37 sheep or goats were consumed each day** for a workforce of around 10,000 at the Menkaure pyramid, with grain, beer and other products supplied additionally [69].

Maintaining that slaughter rate would have required herds of **21,900 cattle and 54,750 sheep and goats** on delivery, with about **3,650 herders** — rising to some 18,980 including their families, under 2% of Egypt's estimated population at the time [69]. Those animals would have been spread across Delta villages and driven to Giza for slaughter [69].

AERA's own account of the material is more specific still. The Silo Building Complex functioned as a commissary; its pottery was dominated by broken bread moulds and baking trays, reflecting a busy bakery operating over 4,300 years ago [70]. Millers knelt at querns to grind emmer or barley; by one estimate a miller could produce a household's daily bread in about three hours [70]. Plant remains from eight areas across the settlement indicate grain arrived already cleaned, with only a final hand-sorting and sieving before milling — meaning the grain was supplied pre-processed from the state storehouses [68]. Pig bones appear in the Silo Building Complex deposits and are read as food obtained outside the state economy, from nearby villagers [70].

On diet specifics, Redding notes that milk and cheese were probably not consumed, owing to transport problems and low milk yield at that time [69]. Meat distribution was stratified: overseers in the north street gatehouse got the most cattle, while people in the galleries ate mainly sheep and goats [69]. Redding's summary of the overall picture is worth preserving, because it inverts the Herodotus story without embellishment: "People were taken care of, and they were well fed when they were down there working, so there would have been an attractiveness to that" [69].

## Payment

Egypt did not use money in this period. The Digital Giza project's formulation is that the workforce was "paid in food, primarily bread and beer" [42]. That is the payment system, and it explains why the settlement's grain infrastructure is as archaeologically prominent as its workshops.

## Why this chapter matters more than it looks

The workforce question is not really about numbers. It is about what the numbers imply about the nature of the state that built the pyramid.

A project of this scale requires: a tax-levying apparatus capable of drawing labour from across a kingdom; a food procurement and distribution system capable of feeding tens of thousands in a desert; a manufacturing supply chain for tools, sledges, timber and rope; a port; a quarry industry; a site administration with written records and seals; a skilled technical corps; and medical provision for injured workers. All of it is attested.

That is the real story of the pyramid's construction: the monument is the visible output of an administrative and logistical apparatus that is itself an achievement of comparable magnitude, and one that we can read in papyri, in sealings, in bread moulds and in cattle bones.
