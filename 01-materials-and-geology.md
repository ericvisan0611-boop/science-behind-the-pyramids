# 01 — Materials and Geology

The Great Pyramid is not one material but three, from three geological settings, each with different properties, different transport requirements, and different degrees of care in the working.

## The mass breakdown

The pyramid holds roughly 2,583,283 m³ of stone and about 6 million tonnes of it. The composition is heavily lopsided toward the cheapest possible option, which is the point: the expensive material is a thin skin.

| Material | Approximate mass | Share of total | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local Giza plateau limestone (core) | 5.0–5.5 million t | 83–92% | Lehner 1997; Aston, Harrell & Shaw 2000, via Copas 2026 [1] |
| Tura white limestone (casing) | ~115,000 t | ~2% | Lehner 1997; Aston, Harrell & Shaw 2000 [1] |
| Aswan red granite (chambers, plugs, beams) | 50,000–70,000 t | ~1% | Lehner 1997; Aston, Harrell & Shaw 2000 [1] |
| Gypsum mortar | ~500,000 t | — | Sustainability problems of the Giza pyramids [2] |

**Source conflict, flagged.** Wikipedia's Great Pyramid article gives granite as 8,000 t, against the 50,000–70,000 t figure from Lehner's 1997 survey volumes as reported by Copas [1]. The lower figure is not reconcilable with a King's Chamber roof of nine granite slabs totalling ~400 t plus five relieving chambers of granite beams in the 25–80 t range plus granite portcullis blocks. This repository uses the Lehner figure and flags the discrepancy rather than splitting the difference.

Across roughly 2.3 million blocks, the average block works out at about 1.13 m³ and 2.9 t [1]. These are *averages over a wildly heterogeneous population* — the pyramid contains blocks from a few hundred kilograms in the upper courses to 80 tonnes in the King's Chamber roof.

## An engineering programme, not a one-off

Khufu's pyramid is the fourth generation of the form, and that matters for how the achievement should be read.

The first pyramid is Djoser's Step Pyramid at Saqqara, built c. 2667-2648 BCE by the architect Imhotep [3][4][5]. It is the earliest colossal stone building in Egypt and the first large-scale use of limestone in construction, replacing mudbrick, which had been the staple building material before it [3]. Its masonry began as a square mastaba whose core of limestone blocks was laid in horizontal beds bound with yellow or red clay, faced with a 2.6 m casing of fine white Tura limestone, with the outer blocks inclined to about 82 degrees [3].

Two details of that early building are directly relevant to everything that follows. First, the weight of the enormous mass was a challenge the builders met by laying the stones at an inward incline to prevent the monument breaking up [4][5]. Second, the structure was revised repeatedly as it rose, through a sequence of distinct construction phases conventionally labelled M1, M2, M3, P1, P1' and P2 after Jean-Philippe Lauer's excavations, before reaching its final stepped form [3]. The pyramid shape was arrived at by experiment, over years, in a building that was repeatedly redesigned on site.

The process continued at Dahshur under Sneferu, where the Bent Pyramid records a change of geometry mid-project and the Red Pyramid that followed it achieved the full slope Khufu would later adopt [6]. The Red Pyramid's alignment error of 8.7 arcminutes [7] puts it in the same family as Khufu's, and the counterclockwise bias is shared.

The point for this repository is that the hard problems of stone architecture had been worked on for roughly two centuries before the Great Pyramid began. Moving a block, bedding a course, corbelling a span, casing a face in smooth white limestone — these were solved skills with a documented institutional memory, not improvisations. A 19th Dynasty inscription at South Saqqara already described Djoser as "the opener of stone", and the Egyptians themselves understood the invention as theirs [5].

## The core: local, rough, and mostly invisible

The great majority of the stone was quarried from the Giza plateau itself and from the large quarry immediately south of the pyramid [1]. It is a nummulitic — fossil-bearing — limestone: the rock is full of the small disc-shaped *Nummulites* that give it its grain. Petrographic work on Sahure's pyramid, the closest comparable study with published mineralogy, found the core stone to be 79% calcite, 18% dolomite and 3% quartz, with the weathered casing stone at 80% calcite, 15% quartz and 3% dolomite [8].

The core was never meant to be seen. Blocks are irregular, roughly dressed, and frequently not in full contact; the interstices are packed with rubble and gypsum mortar. A composite-material analysis drawing on the 1986 French microgravimetric bulk-density survey estimates the interstitial fraction at conservatively in the low-to-mid teens of percent of the constructed volume, with higher values plausible but not securely demonstrated [9]. The care went elsewhere. As one construction-sequence reconstruction puts it, the core masonry is of cruder blocks set with gypsum mortar, sometimes with a fill of broken stone, and the care went into the casing [10].

This is the central material lesson: the pyramid is a *precision envelope around a rough mass*, and confusing the two is the source of most of the popular misreadings.

## The casing: a thin, exact, and now-lost skin

At completion the pyramid was cased entirely in white Tura limestone, set in horizontal courses, carefully fitted and mortared, with outward faces cut to the pyramid's slope and smoothed to a high degree [6]. Each base course was 1.49 m tall; courses shrink to just over one royal cubit (~0.5 m) near the top, where blocks weigh around 500 kg [6].

Two measurements from this casing carry disproportionate weight in arguments about Egyptian precision:

- **Joint opening.** Measurements on the north-eastern casing stones give a mean joint opening of 0.5 mm — about 1/50th of an inch [2]. For a structure whose faces had to hold a geometric envelope across 230 metres, that is the number that matters.
- **Backing stones.** So-called backing stones supported the casing from behind. Unlike core blocks, these were precisely dressed and bound to the casing with mortar [6]. The craftsmanship was spent here, in a layer most visitors will never see and most demolition removed.

The casing was quarried at Tura-Masara, on the east bank of the Nile about 12 km south of modern Cairo, in an extensive system of underground quarries running some 6 km along the river [11]. The supply chain for it is documented in detail in [Chapter 2](02-logistics-and-waterways.md).

Casing blocks survive in *in situ* condition only at the base of the north face, where Vyse's 1837 excavation exposed them [6]. Most of the skin was stripped for other buildings after the Middle Ages; what is visible today is the stepped core.

## The granite: Aswan, and the hardest problem in the building

The granite came from the Aswan region, roughly 900 km south by river [6][1]. It does three jobs:

1. **The King's Chamber** — walls, floor and the nine-slab roof.
2. **The relieving chambers** — their floors and ceilings.
3. **The portcullis / plug system** in the Ascending Passage and the lower shaft.

The blocks that matter are enormous. The nine slabs forming the King's Chamber ceiling total about 400 tonnes, with individual beams in the 25–80 t range; the largest measured at about 5 ft breadth by 7 ft depth by 27 ft length, weighing on the order of 80 t [6][12]. They are set joist-wise — deeper than they are broad — and overhang the chamber walls by roughly 5 ft on each side, so that their ends rest on the masonry rather than on the chamber [6][12].

Granite was also the hardest material to work, and the hardest to transport. It is the single best argument against any account of the pyramid that treats it as a uniform exercise in limestone handling.

## The toolchain

Egypt had no iron, and no wheels or pulleys in the Fourth Dynasty. What it had was better than it is usually given credit for.

**Soft stone — copper and wood.** Copper chisels, saws and drills, wooden mallets, wedges, and stone hammers were the working tools for limestone. The copy for the 2025 excavation of Sahure's pyramid confirms this: the mortar there is 40% calcite, 30% gypsum, 10% quartz and 20% anorthite, and the casing and core are distinguished by calcite/dolomite/quartz ratios rather than by any exotic material [8]. Blocks of roughly 2.5 t were quarried with copper chisels and dolerite pounders in on the order of 45 minutes each in the NOVA replication experiments [13]. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus and Papyrus of Anastasi I both demonstrate that Egyptian scribes could calculate ramp volumes and the number of stones required to build a ramp of given dimensions [14].

**Hard stone — stone tools and fire.** Granite, basalt, gneiss and silicified sandstone were quarried with stone axes and pounders combined with fire-setting, well into the Roman period when iron tools took over [15]. At Widan el-Faras the tool assemblage is dominated by stone axes with contracted necks for hafting, and the blocks themselves are of about 1 m³ — the standard unit for mortuary temple floor paving [15]. Elizabeth Bloxam's survey of Egyptian quarrymen records that quarrying of hard stone "was mainly accomplished using stone tools and fire", with tool marks still clearly visible on the surfaces [16].

**Diamonds, in effect.** The ingenuity was in the abrasive. Hardness is the binding constraint: a copper tool cannot cut quartz, but it can drive a *dolerite* pebble across it. A smooth round dolerite ball — a "pounder" — used with sand as an abrasive is a cutting tool that outperforms the metal it is dressed with. The Egyptians effectively had a diamond without a diamond.

**Timber.** Sledges, rollers, ramps, scaffolding and boats all consumed wood in a country with little of it, which is why Lebanese cedar appears in the Giza material record and why timber procurement is one of the genuine bottlenecks in the Khufu programme [1].

## Mortar: what it did

The mortars of the Great Pyramid are *structural gypsum mortars*, composed of gypsum (Ca(SO₄)(H₂O)₂) with rock fragments of calcite and dolomite, biotite, muscovite and rare quartz, cemented by a fine-grained matrix of gypsum, anhydrite (CaSO₄) and calcite with minor iron oxides [2]. Petrographic and XRD analysis by Klemm and Klemm established lime in Old Kingdom use, but not as a pure lime mortar — rather as gypsum/lime mixtures whose lime content rose between the 3rd and 5th Dynasties and fell again in the 6th [17].

Two practical consequences follow. First, making gypsum mortar required heating to drive off the chemically combined water, which consumes wood — another input on the critical path [1][17]. Second, gypsum is soft and workable, which makes it a poor choice for a levelling medium but an excellent choice for a *lubricating* one: some authors have argued the gypsum mortar's purpose was less to bond the joints than to let the block settle into position precisely, with the real bonding to the casing coming from the backing stones [17].

The core's interstices were filled with rubble and gypsum mortar; the casing's joints were tight to a fraction of a millimetre [2]. Those are different construction philosophies in the same building, and they are the key to understanding it.

## What would settle the remaining material questions

Petrographic sampling with known provenance, across core, backing and casing, with isotopic and grain-boundary characterisation, would either strengthen or demolish the "man-made stone" hypothesis of [Chapter 11](11-contested-hypotheses.md) — which currently rests on a single contested sample from the Lauer collection [18].
