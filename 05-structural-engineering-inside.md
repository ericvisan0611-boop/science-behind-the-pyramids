# 05 — Internal Structural Engineering

Inside the pyramid, the builders were doing something that is easy to miss because it is not visible: they were designing load paths. The Grand Gallery, the relieving chambers and the King's Chamber roof are three distinct solutions to the same problem, and they are the strongest internal evidence that this was engineering rather than masonry.

## The internal system

Eight principal internal elements are recognised [52]:

1. The original entrance with its descending passage
2. The forced entrance cut by al-Ma'mun's workmen
3. The Subterranean Chamber, cut into bedrock and left unfinished
4. The Grand Gallery
5. The Queen's Chamber
6. The King's Chamber
7. The five relieving chambers above it
8. The shafts

The interior is a system, not a set of rooms. Every element in it does work.

## The King's Chamber

The chamber is faced entirely in granite and measures 10.47 m east–west by 5.23 m north–south, with a flat ceiling 5.8 m above the floor [6][53]. Its four walls are built of exactly 100 stones [34]. At its west end stands an empty, lidless granite sarcophagus which is slightly wider than the passage that gives access to it — meaning it had to be placed inside the chamber *while the pyramid was being built* [54].

The roof is the structural crux. It is formed by **nine granite beams** totalling about **400 tonnes**, each running north–south, set joist-wise with their depth greater than their breadth, and **overhanging the chamber walls by roughly 5 ft on each side** so that the beam ends bear on the surrounding masonry rather than on the chamber [6][12]. The largest beam measured is about 5 ft in breadth, 7 ft in depth and 27 ft long, weighing on the order of 80 t [12].

A flat granite roof of that mass, under the superincumbent masonry of a 146.6 m pyramid, would crack. It did crack. All the roof beams show cracks, consistent with the chamber having settled 2.5–5 cm [6].

## The relieving chambers

Above the King's Chamber roof sit five low compartments, named bottom to top for their discoverers: **Davison's Chamber, Wellington's Chamber, Nelson's Chamber, Lady Arbuthnot's Chamber and Campbell's Chamber** [6][12].

Their history is worth a sentence because it is also a history of how the knowledge was lost and regained. Nathaniel Davison is credited with discovering the lowest chamber in 1763, a French merchant named Maynard having informed him of it [6]. The upper four were inaccessible — no access shafts existed for them — until 1837, when Howard Vyse found a crack in the ceiling of the first chamber, inserted a long reed, and used gunpowder with boring rods to open a tunnel upward through the masonry [6]. Vyse's teams, assisted by the French engineer Joseph Bonomi and the Italian explorer Giovanni Belzoni, cleared the chambers in a campaign of deliberate destruction [12].

Their geometry is the engineering. The granite blocks dividing the chambers have flat bottom sides and roughly shaped top sides, giving each chamber an irregular floor but a flat ceiling — with the exception of the uppermost chamber, which has a **pointed limestone roof** [6][12]. Vyse himself described them as "hollows or vacancies consequent upon the peculiar construction of the masonry above the King's Chamber", not chambers in any normal sense, and identified them as a structure "intended by the ancient builders to form together a support for the enormous weight of the superincumbent mass of masonry", which at 300+ feet above the King's Chamber would otherwise be "solid enough to preserve for thousands of years" [12].

The load path works like this. The superincumbent mass bears down on the irregular tops of the granite beams. Because those tops are roughly shaped rather than flat, the load is diverted **laterally into the surrounding masonry** — in effect routing it around the King's Chamber instead of onto it. The topmost chamber's gabled limestone roof does the same job at the highest level, where a gable converts vertical thrust into a horizontal force carried by the walls.

The chambers were never meant to be seen, so they were never finished, and a few of the stones still retain masons' marks — one in Campbell's Chamber bears what appears to be the name of a work gang [12]. That graffiti is the most direct physical evidence that this was a construction site, not a shrine.

And they have now been measured, seismically. See [Chapter 6](06-seismic-performance.md).

### A correction worth making

The frequently repeated claim that the relieving chambers are roofed and floored with "hundreds" of 70-tonne granite beams is an overstatement. The chamber count and the beam weight range are accurate, but documented counts of the granite blocks forming the relieving-chamber ceilings and floors run to roughly three or four dozen, plus the nine separate slabs forming the King's Chamber roof [55]. The order of magnitude in circulation is roughly ten times too high.

## The Grand Gallery

The Grand Gallery is, by the standard of the building, the most sophisticated piece of construction in the pyramid. It continues the slope of the Ascending Passage towards the King's Chamber, running from the 23rd to the 48th course — a rise of 21 m — and measures 46.68 m long and 8.6 m high [6][56].

Its walls are of polished limestone. The base is 4 cubits (2.1 m) wide; after two courses, at a height of 2.29 m, the wall blocks are **corbelled inwards by 6–10 cm on each side**, in **seven steps**, so that at the top the Gallery is only 2 cubits (1.0 m) wide [6]. Corbelling — each course overhanging the one below — is the classic pre-arch way of narrowing a span, and the BBC's architectural assessment calls it the earliest attempt at corbel-vaulting on a colossal scale [57].

### The ratchet ceiling

The roof is the part that reveals the intent. The slabs are **laid at a slightly steeper angle than the floor**, and each stone fits into a slot cut into the top of the gallery, like the teeth of a ratchet [6]. The purpose, as the standard account puts it, was to have each block supported by the wall of the gallery rather than resting on the block beneath it, in order to prevent cumulative pressure [6].

This is a genuinely clever detail. In a normal sloping gallery, the roof slabs stack: each one's weight is carried by the one below, and the accumulation is transferred down the slope. By seating each slab in a slot cut into the wall and letting it overlap the next at a slightly steeper angle, the system converts a stack into an **arch-like ring**: the slabs push on each other and on the corbelled walls, and the thrust goes into the masonry instead of accumulating down the slope.

An independent structural analysis reaches the same conclusion about the mechanics: because the gallery ceiling is constructed in a saw-toothed line with each block set at a greater inclination than those of the floor, the lateral forces of the ceiling are transmitted by intervals to the walls into which the blocks of the roof are plunged [58].

A practical consequence follows for the internal geometry. The floor of the Grand Gallery contains a continuous central trench, and there are regular sockets along the raised bases of both walls [56]. Their function is unknown [56]. They have been proposed as anchoring points for the counterweight and hauling gear of the Houdin model [47], and as socket positions for the ratchet mechanism itself.

### A friction problem in the Gallery

The gallery floor climbs at a mean slope of 26°12′50″ for the Ascending Corridor and Gallery combined [58]. The granite plug blocks, about 5 t each in the lower Ascending Passage, would sit on that slope. With a coefficient of static friction of about 0.45, the net downslope force on such a plug is about 170 kgf — roughly 1,670 N — which is why they must be restrained rather than simply pushed aside [58].

That friction problem has a construction solution that also happens to be a stunning piece of logistics. The plugs in the ascending passage are slightly wider than the passage itself, which means they **cannot have been inserted from outside**. They must have been lowered into position from within the pyramid, from a storage position inside it, quite possibly from the Grand Gallery [57].

The BBC's description is worth quoting for the shape of the problem: the plugs are an inch wider than the entrance, so the plugs must have been lowered into position not from the outside, as was usually the case, but from a storage position within the pyramid itself, perhaps in the Grand Gallery [57].

## The Queen's Chamber and its shafts

The Queen's Chamber sits in the pyramid's body, with a gabled limestone roof [12]. Its two narrow shafts — 0.91 m above the floor in the north and south walls [53] — point outward, and the southern one contains the distinctive corbelled "gothic arch" that diverts the shaft's own weight away from the King's Chamber below [54].

The corbelling is the point. This is not decorative vaulting; it is a load path. The shaft's weight is turned sideways into the surrounding masonry rather than allowed to bear down on the chamber it passes. The same reasoning appears at the top of the Grand Gallery, where a hole in the eastern wall near the roof opens into a short tunnel giving access to Davison's Chamber — that hole is positioned so as not to compromise the gallery's corbelling [6].

## Why the internal design matters for the whole argument

Three separate problems are solved three separate ways inside this building:

- **Overhead load above a flat granite roof:** solved by irregular beam tops and a gabled cap, routing load laterally.
- **Overhead load above a sloping gallery:** solved by a ratchet-seated roof at a steeper angle than the floor, converting a stack into a ring.
- **Load of a vertical shaft passing a chamber:** solved by a corbelled arch that diverts the thrust sideways.

A structure in which voids are load-bearing is a structure whose builders were thinking about force flow, not just about filling space. This is also the part of the pyramid most often overlooked, because the internal chambers are unfashionable compared to the outside.

## The unresolved detail

The Grand Gallery's wall sockets and central trench, the north and south shafts of the King's Chamber (which stop short of the exterior), and the function of the Subterranean Chamber — abandoned unfinished in bedrock — all remain without a settled explanation [52]. They are not mysteries in the rhetorical sense; they are specific, datable, excavatable questions. See [Chapter 10](10-evidence-grading-and-open-questions.md).
