# 09 — Modern Measurement Science

The interior of the pyramid is being mapped with particle physics, radar, ultrasonics and electrical resistance tomography. This chapter covers what has been found, how it was found, and where the frontier actually is as of late 2026.

## Muography: the method

Cosmic rays striking the atmosphere generate muons — massive, short-lived particles that are only partially absorbed by rock. A muon passing through a cavity travels further and is more likely to reach a detector below, so a void registers as an **excess of muons** in a specific direction. Reconstructing the directions of many tracks yields a three-dimensional density map of the structure without touching a single stone.

The method's strengths are exactly what archaeology needs: it is non-invasive, it works at large depths, and it does not require access to the interior. Its weaknesses are equally clear: the detector must be positioned to look *up* through the target region, the reconstruction is statistical rather than photographic, and sensitivity falls with the amount of material between detector and target.

The ScanPyramids project, launched by Egypt's Ministry of Antiquities in October 2015 as a collaboration between Cairo University and the French Institute for Oriental and Near Eastern Archaeology, with the Technical University of Munich, Nagoya University, KEK, CEA, and a wider international group [71], has run three independent detector technologies — nuclear emulsion films, plastic scintillator hodoscopes and gaseous micromegas detectors — and cross-checked their results against each other [72][73].

## The Big Void (2017)

On 2 November 2017, the team published in *Nature* the discovery of a large void above the Grand Gallery, with a cross-section similar to that of the Grand Gallery and a minimum length of **30 metres** [73][74][75].

The claim's strength is in its redundancy. The void was first observed with nuclear emulsion films installed in the Queen's Chamber by Nagoya University, then confirmed with scintillator hodoscopes from KEK in the same chamber, and finally re-confirmed with gas detectors from CEA placed outside the pyramid [73]. The authors describe this as detection "with high confidence by three different muon detection technologies and three independent analyses" [73][74].

The numbers are specific. The statistical significance of the excess exceeded **10 sigma** at the highest difference direction, with combined excesses giving 8.4 sigma for the Grand Gallery and 5.8 sigma for the new void [75]. Triangulation placed the void's centre 40–50 m from the detector positions and about **21 m above ground level** [75]. Its length was determined from the angular spread of the excess: the structure starts near the centre of the pyramid and ends at an angle whose tangent is 0.8 to the north, giving a main length of approximately 30 m [75].

The team was careful with terminology. The structure was named the "ScanPyramids Big Void" rather than a chamber, because its layout and function were unknown [71]. The paper's own statement is that "although there is currently no information about the intended purpose of this void, these findings show how modern particle physics can shed new light on the world's archaeological heritage" [73][74].

### The dispute

Not everyone accepted the framing. Zahi Hawass, then chairman of the ScanPyramids scientific committee, publicly criticised the announcement: "The pyramid is full of voids and that does not mean there is a secret chamber or a new discovery," adding that he had met team members who "showed us their conclusions, and we informed them this is not a discovery" [76].

The scientific content was unaffected — the void was detected at high confidence by three independent techniques [73]. What was contested was the *framing*: whether a density anomaly is a "discovery" in the archaeological sense. The ScanPyramids team's caution in calling it an anomaly rather than a chamber was, in retrospect, the right call, and the 2023 North Face Corridor episode is what vindicated it: there, muography predicted a real passage, and a 5 mm endoscope confirmed it visually [77].

## The North Face Corridor (2023)

The second corridor was found behind the "Chevron" — the assembly of limestone blocks above the original entrance on the north face.

The trail runs: in 2016, nuclear emulsion films in the Descending Corridor detected an unexpected muon excess above it, suggesting an elongated void of at least 5 m oriented north–south [77]. Between 2019 and 2020, CEA placed real-time muon detectors in the same position [77]. And in 2023, the team combined muography with **ground-penetrating radar** and **ultrasonic testing**, published as two papers on the same day, and found the joint opening behind the lower chevron blocks into which a **5 mm diameter endoscope** could be passed [77][78].

On **24 February 2023**, the team showed the corridor to the Scientific Committee [77]. On **2 March 2023**, two scientific papers and the first images were released [77].

The dimensions are the point, because two independent instrument groups measured them separately and agreed:

| Parameter | Nagoya (emulsion) | CEA (gaseous detectors) |
|---|---|---|
| Width W | 2.02 ± 0.06 m | 1.87 ± 0.11 m |
| Height H | 2.18 ± 0.17 m | 1.86 ± 0.12 m |
| Length L | 9.06 ± 0.07 m | 9.23 ± 0.48 m |
| North–south X | 0.84 ± 0.05 m | 0.45 ± 0.22 m |
| East–west Y | 0.03 ± 0.04 m | −0.07 ± 0.11 m |
| Altitude Z | 0.72 ± 0.13 m | 1.34 ± 0.53 m |
| Slope α | −0.3 ± 1.5° | — |

Source: Procureur et al., *Nature Communications* 14, 1144 (2023) [77]. The corridor's north end starts 0.8 m behind the Chevron and ends 9.1 m southward. A horizontal corridor is favoured, with a transverse section around 2.0 × 2.0 m [77].

The endoscope showed a gabled, saddle-vaulted ceiling formed by limestone slabs meeting at an angle, with rough walls and an uneven floor — functional rather than ceremonial [79][80]. The published analyses also note that the NFC does not appear to connect to the Big Void, though a connecting corridor of less than 1.0 m cannot be completely ruled out from these measurements [77].

## Independent confirmation (2025)

Two further papers in *Scientific Reports* closed the loop with conventional geophysics:

- **Schumacher et al. (2025)**, *Sci Rep* 15, 9275, presented a multimodal image fusion using **GPR, ultrasonics and electrical resistivity tomography**, and concluded with "concluding evidence that reflectors 'A' and 'B' are not a result of a thin block joint but due to the presence of a significant air-filled void, i.e. the SP-NFC, located behind Blocks 3 and 4" [81].
- **Pugacheva et al. (2025)**, *Sci Rep* 15, 41187, inverted ERT data and reported the SP-NFC with average dimensions of approximately 2.5 × 2.5 m, starting at a depth of around 1 m and extending at least 2 m into the pyramid [82].

The Technical University of Munich's project listing shows the 2025–2026 corpus continuing: *Detection of Hidden Voids in the Giza Pyramids* (NDT-CE 2025) [72]; material characterisation of stone surfaces in the Khufu inner chambers (2026) [72]; ultrasonic testing with shear wave arrays on the lower block rows of the King's Chamber (2026) [72]; and 2026 ECNDT proceedings on NDT, numerical simulation, image fusion and virtual reality for the pyramid, plus a study revealing hidden structures in the eastern wall of the Menkaure pyramid [72].

### Menkaure

In November 2025, the ScanPyramids team announced **two air-filled anomalies on the eastern side of the Menkaure pyramid**, found by combining muon and other data. The first is about 4.5 ft behind the outer facade, measuring roughly 3 ft tall by 5 ft wide; the second sits almost 4 ft behind the facade, roughly 3 ft tall and a little more than 2 ft wide [83]. The team called the hypothesis of another entrance "very plausible" [83].

This is a good example of the method working as intended: the anomalies were found in the world's third pyramid, in a region that had been surveyed for a century, by a technique that does not require entering the monument.

## The 2026 frontier

Three developments mark the current state of play, and all three are worth stating precisely because the popular coverage of them is unreliable.

**The Big Void's interior has still never been seen.** No access route to it is known and no instrument has been inside it. Its shape and function remain unknown, and further muography observations from new viewpoints are still required before functional inferences can be drawn [71][73]. The 2023 endoscope reached the North Face Corridor, which is a different structure [77].

**A robot reached a barrier in a narrow shaft.** In August 2026, reporting described a University of Leeds team — Prof. Rob Richardson with Scout and DARUSoft — sending a ~5 kg robot up a shaft 20 cm square and roughly 60 m long, on a project begun in 2020 after a challenge from Hong Kong dentist and inventor Dr. Tze Chuen Ng. The robot reached the end of the passage and found what was described as a solid wall or a sealed doorway [84]. No open room was reported and no chamber was demonstrated [84][85].

**The "30 m corridor ending in a sealed door" is announced but unpublished.** Zahi Hawass said at the Sharjah International Book Fair on 8 November 2025 that scanning and robotics had located "a new 30-meter-long passageway... which ends at a door that will rewrite a chapter in the history of the pharaohs", to be unveiled in 2026 with a scientific report first [86][87]. As of late 2026, no peer-reviewed publication of that corridor had appeared [88]. In a May 2026 interview he clarified that what the team has is the doors, the two voids and a corridor [88].

**And one retraction, which is the point.** On 10 August 2026, *Remote Sensing* retracted a 2022 paper by Filippo Biondi and Corrado Malanga, *Synthetic Aperture Radar Doppler Tomography Reveals Details of Undiscovered High-Resolution Internal Structure of the Great Pyramid of Giza*, after a reader raised concerns and the journal's editorial office and board "confirmed serious methodological flaws and statistical errors" that undermined the paper's conclusions; the notice recorded that the authors disagreed with the decision [89][90].

The retraction does not touch the muography results. The Big Void and the North Face Corridor rest on detector technologies and cross-checks that the withdrawn paper had no part in [85]. What the retraction does establish is a calibration for how fast a "hidden city" headline travels: the paper's reconstructions of 20 structures inside and beneath the pyramid were never confirmed by excavation or on-site geophysical survey, and the team's later claims of shafts and chambers beneath Khafre — the claims that produced the "underground city" headlines — were never submitted for peer review at all [89][85].

## Why this matters

The muography programme has done something that no amount of textual argument could: it has established that the pyramid's interior is *not* fully known, and that the parts of it that are known are known to centimetre-scale precision, with uncertainties stated. Three detector technologies, two geophysical methods, and an endoscope have converged on the same geometry from different directions.

It has also sharpened the open question. The Big Void has a cross-section similar to the Grand Gallery, sits directly above it, and is at least 30 m long. The Grand Gallery's own function is disputed — Houdin reads it as housing a counterweight for hoisting the granite beams [47], and the 2025 sliding-ramp proposal reads it as a working ramp for descent [51]. If either reading is right, a void with a Grand Gallery cross-section directly above the Grand Gallery is exactly where a construction system would leave a space.

That remains a hypothesis. The honest position is that the void's *existence* is established to high confidence and its *function* is unknown — and that the leading candidate function, load relief, is the one with the least independent support, because the same argument could equally apply to a counterweight void left by the construction method itself.
