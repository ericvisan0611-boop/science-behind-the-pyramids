# 04 — Ramps and Raising Blocks

Every account of the pyramid's construction has to answer the same question: how did a block get to 140 metres? This is the largest genuine open question in the field, and the interesting part is that every candidate answer fails for a *different*, specific, diagnosable reason.

## The constraint

A block of 2.5 t on a sledge at μ_d = 0.2 needs about 5 kN of pull to move on level ground. On a ramp at gradient θ, the resistive force gains a gravity term:

```
F = m·g·(μ_d·cos θ + sin θ)
```

and the *available* pull from a crew is fixed by human sustained power output — around 75 W per fit worker over a working day [36]. This produces a hard, non-negotiable constraint: the steeper the ramp, the fewer blocks per crew per day, and the steeper it gets, the more the crew must simply lift rather than drag.

A ramp at 1:10 is about 5.7°. A ramp at 1:8 is 7.1°. A ramp at 1:4 is 14°. At Hatnub, the one Old Kingdom haul ramp that has actually been measured, the gradient reached **20%** — about 11.3° — and it was hauled by ropes tied to posts set along the ramp [43][10].

This is why the gradient argument is not merely about material. A ramp's *length* and its *gradient* trade against each other, and the trade sets a hard ceiling on how fast stone can arrive.

## The ramp typology

### The straight ramp

The oldest and simplest proposal: one straight ramp from the ground to the apex, with the pyramid built symmetrically around it.

Arnold's calculation is the standard citation. A ramp holding a **1:10** gradient to the summit requires approximately **8 million m³** of fill — more than three times the volume of the pyramid itself. Reducing the gradient to **1:20** doubles the length to nearly **3 km** [30]. Edwards put it the same way: a ramp would have been about 1.4 km long and required more than three times the volume of material used in the completed pyramid [14].

Two independent failures kill it. The material does not exist at Giza: Klemm and Klemm, and Müller-Römer, find far too little ramp debris to account for a ramp on this scale [10]. And the space does not exist either — Rigby notes there is simply no room on the Giza plateau for a ramp over a mile long, which leaves the south side as the only candidate face, where no remains have been found [14]. The Wikipedia summary of the field is blunt: the large straight ramp is "routinely discredited on functional grounds for its massive size, lack of archaeological evidence, huge labor cost, and other problems" [44].

Straight ramps were almost certainly used for the **lower** courses. The argument is not about whether ramps existed; it is about whether one ramp could have gone all the way up.

### The wrapping or spiral ramp

Spiralling the ramp around the exterior removes the length problem and the material problem at once, and it explains the absence of ramp remains: the ramp's own blocks are recycled into the upper pyramid once the internal chambers are complete [14][45].

It fails on two grounds that are easier to check.

*Corner geometry.* A long sledge cannot turn a 90° corner in a narrow spiral, and the problem compounds with every course [44]. Lehner's team found this the hard way at their small pyramid, where the post-and-pivot corner manoeuvre failed outright once the block was two feet off the ground [38].

*Survey control.* This is the more interesting objection, and it is an engineering objection rather than a logistical one. The pyramid's faces had to be laid out to an accuracy of a few arcminutes and to a level of a fraction of an inch across 13.1 acres [6]. A ramp spiralling up the exterior hides the corners and blocks the sight lines needed to maintain that geometry. The npj *Heritage Science* computational study of 2026 states it as a design constraint on a family of models: "zig-zag or side ramps obstruct survey control and block perimeter access", and "external spiral designs impede corner visibility and turning" [46]. The same study notes that most ramp theories obscure the faces, forcing post-removal casing that complicates precision alignment and lacks archaeological support [46].

### The internal ramp

Jean-Pierre Houdin's proposal is the most fully engineered of the candidates, and it deserves a serious account rather than a caricature.

The scheme [47][48][45]:

1. A **conventional external straight ramp** builds the lower portion. Houdin's models place this transition at **43 m**, at which point roughly **65% of the pyramid's volume** is complete [48][49]. A 2026 engineering analysis puts the sub-43 m volume at 1,679,134 m³ of 2,583,283 m³ [48].
2. Above that level, an **internal spiral ramp** carved into the core delivers blocks to successively higher courses, in a pyramid-within-a-pyramid about 10–15 m beneath the outer skin [49].
3. At each turn, a **corner notch** of roughly 10 m² is left open in the edge of the construction, housing a device that lifts and rotates each 2.5 t block so that eight men can drag it onto the next ramp leg [47].
4. The Grand Gallery is reinterpreted as housing a **giant counterweight** that hoisted the five ~60 t granite beams into position above the King's Chamber [47][50].
5. The material of the demolished external ramp is **cut smaller and recycled** into the upper pyramid [45] — which is why no ramp remains.

The engineering case for the ramp's material economy is strong. An internal ramp of 2 × 2 m cross-section running a total path of about 1,600 m occupies only ~6,400 m³ — **0.25% of the pyramid's volume** [48]. Dassault Systèmes put 14 engineers and roughly 5,000 hours of CATIA/SIMULIA simulation into testing the scheme [45].

The criticisms are equally specific.

David Jeffreys of University College London called the internal spiral hypothesis "far-fetched and horribly complicated" in 2007 [47]. John Baines of Oxford said he was "suspicious of any theory that seeks to explain only how the Great Pyramid was built" [47]. Craig B. Smith, in *How the Great Pyramid Was Built*, objected that it adds unnecessary complexity to what were, in his view, practical problems — and pointed out that there is no evidence internal ramps were used in any pyramid built before or after the Great Pyramid [45].

The 2026 npj *Heritage Science* computational framework puts the evidential objection more precisely: internal spirals "assume undocumented internal corridors and struggle to match muography results", while also noting that non-detection of internal spirals must be interpreted within method sensitivity and coverage limits, so current data "neither confirm nor exclude edge-integrated backfill" [46].

There is one piece of genuine positive evidence. An **internal ramp survives at the Abu Gurob sun temple**, built for Ni-Userre in the Fifth Dynasty roughly a century after Khufu and excavated by Borchardt in 1898 [14]. That establishes the technique existed. It does not establish that it was used at Giza, and Rigby is careful to note that it is the *only* such physical evidence — that "adds more physical evidence to the theory than simply the notch and the L-shaped room", but the notch is what the theory predicts, not what the evidence proves [14].

### A recent re-reading of the internal passages

A 2025 paper in npj *Heritage Science* proposes something genuinely different: that the Grand Gallery, the Ascending Passage and the Descending Passage were **sliding ramps** rather than passages, with blocks sent down as counterweights on a wooden sledge, and the Antechamber acting as a pulley-like mechanism using wooden logs [51]. A companion 2026 analysis assesses the proposal as mechanically sound for blocks up to about 10 t and as operating in parallel with external methods, while noting that a single counterweight would be impractical and that multiple smaller counterweights in sequence would be needed [48].

This is a live, peer-reviewed, falsifiable proposal in the current literature. It is not established. It is included here because "the frontier" is part of what this repository documents, and because it illustrates that the internal passages are still being read for function.

### Back-and-forth ramps

A 2026 preprint proposes a system of two external ramps at **1:8** (about 7°) climbing the east and south faces, with the east ramp 11 m wide to the King's Chamber floor and 9 m wide above 65.9 m, carrying two lanes of sledges below that height [10].

Its accounting is worth reading because it is the most complete attempt yet to close the book:

- The tiers and ramps of the base case hold **268,800 m³** of block — **10.4%** of the pyramid's 2.59 million m³ [10].
- The wide east ramp costs **72,700 m³** on its own, because on thin tiers the whole terrace must be as deep as the ramp is wide [10].
- Two rubble ramps at the lowest courses add about 7,700 m³ of waste from cutting bedrock [10].
- And the conclusion that matters: **"One ramp alone is never enough: two are the minimum"** [10].

The same preprint states the problem in its clearest form: "A straight ramp to the top would hold about as much stone as the pyramid. A spiral ramp on the faces hides the corners, which the builders had to sight to keep the geometry. An internal ramp is narrow and turns at right angles. And no large ramp debris survives at Giza." [10]

It also proposes a **finishing-from-the-top** sequence — 35 tiers of dry-laid block standing against the four faces as mastabas, with the core laid from the centre outward, the backing and casing placed last as the outer ring, and each course finished with its casing as the ramp advances [10]. That is an elegant answer to the observation that the core is rough and the casing is exact: finish each course completely as you go, from the top down, rather than cladding at the end.

### The edge-integrated ramp

The 2026 computational framework evaluates a family of ramp geometries and settles on an "edge-integrated ramp" as the most material-efficient external variant [46]. Its framing is notable for its epistemic care: a ramp of this type "leaves no permanent external structures, so direct archeological traces are scarce" — which it explicitly labels "a falsifiable prediction, not evidence" [46]. It estimates that a straight frontal ramp to 30–40 m would require about 0.24–0.56 million m³ of fill [46].

## The one measured ramp

The Hatnub alabaster quarry, excavated by the IFAO and the University of Liverpool, contains the only Old Kingdom haul ramp that has been directly measured [43][10]. Its reconstruction: a **central ramp between two staircases, 7.8 m wide in all, about 80 m long, rising to 20%**, with **post holes along its sides** [43][10]. Brichieri-Colombi reads the posts as **bollards** for guiding or hitching sledges [10].

This is an extraordinary piece of evidence because it is a ramp of Khufu's reign — a working ramp, of the right date, for the right kind of task, with the physical traces of how sledges were managed on it. It establishes the *technique*. It does not establish the *geometry at Giza*, because a quarry ramp is a fundamentally different problem: a fixed, narrow, steep haul from a single face, not a moving, wide, shallow ramp wrapping a growing mass.

## What the evidence does and does not constrain

The Egyptological texts do not settle the ramp question, but they do show that the problem was understood as a calculable one. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus and the Papyrus of Anastasi I both show Egyptian scribes calculating ramp volumes and the number of stones required to build a ramp of given dimensions [14]. The problem was posed mathematically, in writing, by people who could do arithmetic on ramps. That is a far cry from a mystery, and it is a point the popular account consistently loses.

The genuine state of play is this, and it is the position the 2026 capacity-constraints analysis takes: straight external ramps, spiral ramps, internal ramps and combination systems have all been proposed, the debate has not been resolved, and whatever system was used had to sustain the throughput required while accommodating the increasing height and decreasing course volume of the upper pyramid [1].

## What would settle it

Three measurements would do it, and none has been performed:

1. **Microgravimetry or density mapping over the whole pyramid volume.** A ramp void, or a spiral of them, should produce a density anomaly. Houdin's team proposed exactly this in 2007 [45]. The 2026 framework notes that a zero-footprint system would leave only "subtle density anomalies" — which is to say, a specific, falsifiable signature at a specific resolution [46].
2. **Direct excavation of the space between the Grand Gallery and the core.** The Grand Gallery's relation to a spiral ramp is the single most informative location in the building.
3. **Recovered ramp debris at a scale that matches the models.** The absence of large ramp debris is currently the strongest argument against external ramps, and the strongest argument against it is also its weakest point: the explanation that the material was recycled "has itself not been independently verified" [1].
