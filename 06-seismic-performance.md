# 06 — Seismic Performance

The Great Pyramid has stood for more than 4,600 years. Egypt is not a low-seismic country. A 2026 study gives a quantitative account of why the structure has survived.

## The seismic record

The pyramid has weathered serious earthquakes without major structural damage, including one in **1847 with an estimated magnitude of 6.8** and another in **1992 with a magnitude of 5.8** [52]. There is evidence of older ones too: Vyse attributed the settlement and cracking of the entire King's Chamber — a slight tilt toward the south-west corner, with every beam in the immediate roof broken across near the south wall, such that the immense granite ceiling is held up "solely by sticking and thrusting" — to a severe earthquake in **908 CE** [12].

That last point is instructive in its own right. The chamber did not fail. It cracked, tilted, and held. That is the definition of a structure that is being asked to do something.

## The frequency mismatch

The method used is **ambient noise surveying with horizontal-to-vertical spectral ratio (HVSR) analysis**. Ambient noise is the constant, low-level vibration of the ground from wind, ocean swell, traffic and microseisms; measuring how it translates through a structure reveals the structure's fundamental frequency without needing an earthquake or an artificial source.

In 2026, a team from Egypt's National Research Institute of Astronomy and Geophysics and colleagues published the results of a survey at **37 measurement points** distributed across the pyramid's internal chambers, its construction blocks and the adjacent soil [52]. The findings:

- The pyramid exhibits a uniform fundamental frequency of **2.0–2.6 Hz**, averaging about **2.3 Hz**, across all structural elements — a level of dynamic homogeneity the authors describe as exceptional [52].
- The surrounding soil vibrates at approximately **0.6 Hz** [52][59].
- These two values are far apart, which **prevents resonance amplification through soil–structure interaction** [52].
- The relative amplification factor is 1.0 at ground level and rises with elevation, reaching a maximum of about **4** at the King's Chamber elevation [52].
- The exception is the **pressure-relieving chambers**, at 48.86–61.07 m, where the amplification factor drops back to roughly 3 — the voids demonstrably interrupt the seismic response [52].
- The **seismic vulnerability index** K_g for the foundation soils is **8.2**, well below the value of 20 that would indicate elevated site vulnerability [52].

The mechanism is straightforward. Resonance requires that the driving frequency of the ground match the natural frequency of the structure. At 0.6 Hz driving and 2.3 Hz responding, the two are well separated, so seismic energy transfers inefficiently and destructive amplification does not build up [59].

## The authors' own caveat

This is the part worth preserving carefully, because it is where a great deal of popular writing departs from the paper.

The study is explicit that the measured frequency mismatch "likely reduces resonance effects during earthquakes" and that this is a *mechanical consequence* of the geometry — a wide base, a low height-to-base ratio, a firm bedrock foundation and a regular mass distribution [52][59].

It is equally explicit that this does not establish intent: "Any suggestion of intentional seismic optimization by ancient Egyptian architects remains purely speculative and cannot be substantiated by geophysical measurements alone" [52]. The authors describe the monument as "intelligently balanced and well-tuned for stability", with good damping, solid torsion resistance, low height-to-base ratio, and centre of mass and centre of resistance located at or close to the same point [52] — but as properties the structure happens to have.

The distinction matters and it is worth being pedantic about, because the temptation to slide from "the pyramid is remarkably earthquake-resistant" to "the Egyptians designed it to withstand earthquakes" is exactly the move that separates measurement from storytelling. The first is a measurement. The second is an attribution, and it has no evidence.

## The K_g index, correctly stated

The K_g = 8.2 figure is frequently quoted as though it were a measure of the pyramid's safety. It is not. It is an index of **soil** vulnerability, and the study itself says so: the index assesses soil vulnerability only, not the structural safety of the entire stone monument, and current evidence does not support using soil-based K_g values to evaluate large stone structures, since that would require a methodology linking soil parameters to internal monument dynamics [52].

So: K_g = 8.2 means the foundation soils are not themselves a weak link. It is not a safety certificate for the pyramid, and it should not be presented as one.

## Why the relieving chambers show up seismically

The relieving chambers are usually explained in terms of static load — diverting the weight of the masonry above the King's Chamber [6]. The HVSR survey adds a second, independent reason for them to exist: they also reduce the seismic amplification measured at the highest points of the structure [52][60].

That is an interesting convergence. Two unrelated disciplines — static structural analysis and ambient-noise geophysics — both find the same voids doing the same work, using entirely different measurements.

It also strengthens the case that the chambers were designed rather than accidental. Houdin's team reached the opposite conclusion from the geometry: that the architect concerned about cracks cutting into the sealed space above the burial chamber cut a tunnel to assess the damage and then filled the cracks with plaster as a tell-tale that would indicate if they were widening [47]. The beams held and the pyramid was completed.

That claim is more speculative than the seismic result — it depends on reading the plaster repairs as diagnostic rather than remedial — but it is the kind of hypothesis that becomes testable once the plaster is sampled and dated.

## Geometry as passive protection

The structural advantages that the 2026 study relies on are all consequences of shape rather than of material or technique [59]:

- The base spans over 755 ft, giving a low height-to-base ratio that resists tipping [59].
- The structure is highly symmetrical, distributing load evenly [59].
- It sits on a firm bedrock foundation [59].
- The weight is concentrated towards the ground and distributed across the base, so the structure is stable in its own mass [60].

None of these require an ancient technology that we cannot identify. They require only that the builders chose a form with a low, wide profile and a symmetric envelope — which is, in fairness, what a pyramid *is*.

## What the study does not establish

- It does not establish that the builders knew about resonance or set out to exploit the frequency mismatch. The authors rule that out explicitly [52].
- It does not date the damage in the King's Chamber to a specific earthquake. The 908 CE attribution is Vyse's, from the 19th century, not a product of this survey.
- It does not model the whole structure's response to a major nearby rupture. The amplification factors are relative, measured with ambient noise, and the study does not attempt a full nonlinear dynamic analysis.
- It does not tell us about the structural behaviour of the core, which is roughly interstitial rubble-and-mortar mass with irregular blocks not always in contact [9]. The HVSR measurements inside the pyramid were taken in the chambers, in construction blocks and in the soil — not through the core volume as such.

That last limitation is the most consequential. The pyramid's seismic performance is the performance of a rough composite core with a precision envelope and a set of engineered voids inside it, and the 2026 survey characterises the dynamic properties of the chambers and blocks it could reach rather than of the bulk mass.

## Grade summary

| Claim | Grade | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamental frequency 2.0–2.6 Hz (avg ~2.3) inside the pyramid | A | 37-point HVSR survey [52] |
| Surrounding soil frequency ~0.6 Hz; mismatch prevents resonance amplification | A | Same survey, with independent reporting [52][59] |
| Relative amplification 1 at ground, ~4 at King's Chamber elevation; relieving chambers reduce it | A | Same survey [52] |
| Foundation soil vulnerability index K_g = 8.2 (low) | A | Same survey, with the authors' own limit on its use [52] |
| Pyramid survived the 1847 (~M6.8) and 1992 (~M5.8) earthquakes without major damage | C | Compiled from historic records, no damage survey attached [52] |
| The King's Chamber tilt and beam cracking are attributable to the 908 CE earthquake | C | Vyse's 19th-century attribution, consistent with observed cracking [12] |
| The builders intentionally optimised the structure for seismic performance | E | Explicitly unsupportable from geophysical data alone [52] |

## Where this goes next

The 2026 team plans to repeat measurements at locations that "gave little anomalies", expecting the results to affirm the Khufu Pyramid as "an architectural marvel and a testament to ancient seismic engineering principles relevant to modern geoheritage conservation" [60]. The follow-up measurement is the one that matters: if the frequency distribution turns out to be as uniform as the first campaign suggests, the description of the pyramid as a deliberately tuned structure becomes considerably harder to sustain as coincidence.
