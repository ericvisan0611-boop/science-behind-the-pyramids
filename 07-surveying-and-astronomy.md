# 07 — Surveying and Astronomy

The pyramid's most quoted precision figure — alignment to the cardinal points within about four arcminutes — is often used to imply mystery. In fact it is the opposite. It is a metrology problem with a bounded answer, and several candidate solutions have already been tested in the field.

## What "four arcminutes" means

One degree of arc is 60 arcminutes, and one arcminute is 60 arcseconds. Four arcminutes is one-fifteenth of a degree. At the Great Pyramid's distance, that is a directional error of roughly **0.8 m over 230 m** [6].

The measurements behind the figure are solid and recent. In February 2015, the Glen Dash Foundation for Archaeological Research and AERA, with permission from the Egyptian Ministry of Antiquities, carried out a comprehensive resurvey of the pyramid's base, remapping four control monuments and identifying **84 points** around the periphery where the original casing baseline survives [61][62]. The baseline was then defined as the place where the outer lower edge of the casing stones met the platform [61].

The results, with error bounds, are the most precise statement available:

- The **west side** gave a mean angle of **4′21″ west of true north**, with a 95% confidence interval of 3′45″ to 4′56″, from 30 points of casing evidence along 49 m [61].
- The **north side** gave a predicted line at **2′30″ north of due east**, with a 95% confidence interval of 2′07″ to 2′53″, from 16 points over 51 m [61].
- The side lengths came out at 230.329 m (north), 230.334 m (east), 230.384 m (south) and 230.407 m (west) as means, each with minimum and maximum bounds spanning roughly 0.15 m [61].
- The four corner intersections closed with residuals of 0.025–0.103 m [61].

The survey also established that about **17% of the original casing survives** [61].

Earlier benchmark: Flinders Petrie's theodolite survey of 1882–83 found the casing aligned to about **3′43″ ± 6″** west of true north [63]. Dash's summary places Khufu at better than four arcminutes, Khafre at about six arcminutes and Sneferu's Red Pyramid at Dahshur at 8.7 arcminutes [7]. The mean error across all three is **−6.2 arcminutes** — a slight *counterclockwise* rotation from true north [7].

That shared sign is the interesting detail. Three pyramids, two sites, one dynasty family, and a systematic rotation in the same direction. A random surveying error would not do that.

## Candidate methods, and which have been tested

Glen Dash's field testing of 2015 compiled results for four proposed techniques: the pole star method, the circumpolar star method, the simultaneous transit method, and the Indian circle (solar gnomon) method [64].

His verdict is careful and honest: "The available evidence neither confirms nor refutes the hypothesis that the casings of the Fourth Dynasty pyramids were aligned using the simultaneous transit method. The other methods also have the requisite accuracy." [64] On the descending passages at Dahshur, there is reason to believe the pole star method was used [64].

### The equinoctial solar gnomon

Dash's 2017 paper proposed a variant of the solar gnomon method that he calls the "equinoctial solar gnomon method", and it is the most elegant of the candidates [7]. The method is the "Indian circle": drive a vertical rod into the ground and mark the tip of its shadow through the day. On an equinox, the shadow-tip path becomes a nearly straight east–west line, and a taut string stretched between two marked points gives the cardinal direction directly.

Dash tested it himself on **22 September 2016** in Pomfret, Connecticut [7][65]. His two experimental lines had a mean error of **−7.3 arcminutes** — a slight counterclockwise rotation [7]. The mean error of the three pyramids is −6.2 arcminutes [7]. The magnitudes and directions agree.

Dash is careful about the limits of his own claim. "The equinoctial solar gnomon method appears to be workable," he writes; "It joins the list of methods the Egyptians might have used to align their pyramids" [7]. And, more importantly: "As to the methods they actually did use, the Egyptians, unfortunately, left us few clues. No engineering documents or architectural plans have been found that give technical explanations demonstrating how the ancient Egyptians aligned any of their temples or pyramids" [7].

That sentence is the crux of the whole chapter. The method is underdetermined. The precision is not.

### The simultaneous transit method

Kate Spence's proposal uses the moment when two circumpolar stars culminate simultaneously, which happens at a specific right ascension and declination that drifts over centuries — meaning it would have been different for the Old Kingdom than today [64][7]. This is the most interesting candidate historically, because it explains a *systematic error of a known sign and magnitude* rather than an arbitrary one, and because the error is time-dependent in a way that can be checked against construction dates.

### The circumpolar star method

Using a pair of circumpolar stars, including Kochab, to bisect the pole. Tested in the field by Joseph Dorner [7]. Has the requisite accuracy [64].

### The pole star method

Petrie's original proposal, using the two stars at the foot of the Little Dipper to bisect the pole [7]. The pole star's position in 2600 BCE differs from its position now, which is the method's main complication and also the reason it can be checked against dating.

### What the Trial Passages might be for

The Giza plateau contains the remains of two "trial passages" — short passageways cut into bedrock southeast of the Great Pyramid, deliberately misaligned by about a quarter of a degree east of true north [63].

Gerard Lightbody has proposed that the Trial Passages were designed for accurate observation of the circumpolar stars in advance of the pyramid's construction, with observation devices of the type Dash describes, establishing an accurate north–south reference line for the plateau that was then translated laterally to set out the east edge of the Great Pyramid [63]. The reasoning: the Trial Passages' quarter-degree misalignment is a *deliberate* offset, and a two-step procedure — a rapid alignment to north before cutting the passages, then a prolonged stellar observation period to refine it — would have improved the final accuracy by a factor of four over the ~1/15 to 1/16 of a degree actually achieved [63].

The hypothesis is explicitly labelled hypothetical by its author: "there is almost no written or physical evidence of the methods used to achieve the alignment, and so the proposed arrangement described in this article remains hypothetical" [63].

But note what the argument requires: an observer with excellent eyesight, a prolonged observation period perhaps stretching into several years, and very careful handling of the instrument's small components [63]. That is a real cost, and it is the kind of cost that explains a system rather than a single-shot alignment.

## Base levelling

Orientation is only half the metrology. The base had to be level to a tolerance that no contemporary construction matched.

Flinders Petrie's observation was that the Egyptians levelled the platform by cutting a grid of shallow trenches into the bedrock, flooding them with water, and reducing the intervening "islands" of stone to the required height [57]. The method is self-checking: water finds its own level, so every island is cut to exactly the same elevation, and the residual error is the saw's cut tolerance.

The reported outcome is that the site was levelled **within a fraction of an inch over the entire 13.1-acre base** [6] — an area of 5.3 hectares. The 2015 resurvey's corner-closure residuals of 0.025–0.103 m [61] and the alignment precision above are the modern confirmation that Petrie's reading was right.

## The seked and the cubit

The Egyptian design system is worth noting because it shows the pyramid was dimensioned, not eyeballed. The completed design dimensions are given as **280 royal cubits high** by **440 cubits** at each base side, with a *seked* of **5½ palms** — a slope of 51°50′40″ [6]. A seked is a slope expressed as a ratio of rise to horizontal run, which is a piece of practical surveying mathematics rather than symbolism.

The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus and the Papyrus of Anastasi I both show Egyptian scribes solving problems of ramp volume and the number of stones required for a ramp of given dimensions [14]. The construction project was administered in units.

## Why this chapter is not a mystery

Three things combine here.

First, the precision is now measured, not asserted — with confidence intervals, from 84 surveyed baseline points, by a survey that explicitly re-examined and improved on Petrie's 1882–83 theodolite work [61][63].

Second, the candidate methods have been field-tested and shown to be capable of the required accuracy, with one of them — the equinoctial gnomon — demonstrated to produce the same magnitude *and direction* of error as the three pyramids actually exhibit [7][64].

Third, and decisively, no engineering record survives. There is no construction papyrus that says how the base was set out. And I.E.S. Edwards noted that long ago, as has every subsequent commentator [63].

So the honest position is: *how precisely* the pyramid was aligned is not in doubt and is now quantified; *how* it was aligned is underdetermined among at least four methods that all work, with the strongest circumstantial support going to techniques that would produce exactly the observed counterclockwise bias. The residual uncertainty is a genuine historical gap — the Egyptians' own records did not survive — rather than a failure of imagination on our part.
