# 02 — Logistics and Waterways

The engineering questions about the pyramid are hard. The logistics questions are harder, because they dominate.

## The scale of the problem

The Great Pyramid consumed about 6 million tonnes of stone in roughly 20–26 years [6][1]. That is on the order of 800 tonnes placed every day, or more than 12 blocks an hour, day and night, for two decades [6].

What makes this tractable is that the mass is not distributed as the popular image suggests. Around 83–92% of it came from the Giza plateau itself [1]. The genuinely long-distance problem is a small fraction of the total:

- **Tura white limestone** for the casing: ~115,000 t, about 12 km by river from the east bank [1].
- **Aswan granite** for the chambers: 50,000–70,000 t, about 900 km by river [6][1].
- **Timber** for sledges, rollers, ramps and boats, largely from the Levant [1].
- **Food, fuel and people**, in quantities that make the pyramid a consumer rather than a producer of wealth [19].

So the pyramid is not a problem of moving 6 million tonnes across Egypt. It is a problem of moving perhaps 300,000–400,000 tonnes a substantial distance, and 5.5 million tonnes a short one, while feeding tens of thousands of people in a desert.

## Merer's logbook

In 2013, a French mission under Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard excavated papyri at Wadi al-Jarf, on the Red Sea coast 119 km south of Suez, in front of man-made caves that had stored boats [20]. The best preserved documents, Papyrus Jarf A and B, are the logbooks of an official titled *inspector* (sḥḏ) named Merer, written in the 26th–27th year of Khufu's reign [20][21]. They are among the oldest written business documents known from ancient Egypt, and they are a production log for the Great Pyramid.

Merer's entries are day-by-day and specific:

- About **40 boatmen** worked under him [20].
- He sailed two or three round trips per ten-day week, each carrying perhaps **30 blocks of 2–3 t** [20].
- That works out to roughly **200 blocks per month** [20].
- A loaded boat carried **70–80 t** [19]; the downstream run to Giza took **two days**, the unloaded return one [19].
- The work was possible only during the annual inundation, in the season the Egyptians called Akhet — June to November [19][22].
- The crew was a *phyle* drawn from a larger group of about 160 men called "the Followers of 'The Uraeus of Khufu is its Prow'" [19].
- The logbook names "the nobleman Ankhhaf" as Director of "the entrance to the Pool of Khufu" [19] — the king’s half-brother, running a staging point on the route.
- A parallel logbook by a scribe named Dedi, and a set of *accounts* covering the movement of bread, beer, cereals and meat, extend the archive to roughly a full year [19][22].

**What this does and does not prove.** Tallet believes the stone Merer carried was for the outer casing, given the logbook's date near the end of Khufu's reign [20]. The diary itself does not specify the destination or purpose of the stone [20]. The strongest reading is narrower and firmer: this is a *documented, scheduled, flood-season logistics operation* with named officials, boat crews, waypoints and accounts — and the pyramid's supply line was administered at a level that generates papyri.

That is the single most important correction the logbook forces. The ancient builders are not a mystery. They are administrators whose paperwork survived.

## The water: the Ahramat Branch

For most of Egypt's history the Giza plateau has been a desert edge. Merer's boats could not have delivered stone to the plateau without a canal system and a harbour, and the geography has been the least well-understood part of the puzzle.

In 2024, Eman Ghoneim, Timothy Ralph and colleagues published the resolution of this using radar satellite imagery (Sentinel-1), TanDEM-X topographic data, geophysical survey and deep soil coring [23]. They identified segments of a major extinct branch of the Nile — the **Ahramat Branch** — running along the foothills of the Western Desert Plateau, exactly where the bulk of Egypt's pyramids are clustered.

The result explains a puzzle that had stood for decades: why the largest pyramid field in Egypt is concentrated on one narrow strip of desert. The branch was at least 64 km long, roughly 0.5 km wide and at least 25 m deep [6][23]. Crucially, many pyramids' **causeways terminate with valley temples at the branch's banks** — and a valley temple positioned at a river landing is a harbour [23].

The paper's conclusion is deliberately modest and correspondingly strong: the Ahramat Branch "played a role in the monuments' construction" and was "simultaneously active and used as a transportation waterway for workmen and building materials to the pyramids' sites" [23]. It was not an incidental geographic feature. It was the reason the pyramids are where they are.

The Ankhhaf reference in Merer's diary — "the entrance to the Pool of Khufu" — and the dike and floodgate work his crew performed are consistent with a managed canal and basin network feeding the construction site from that branch [22].

## A second port: Heit el-Ghurab

The settlement excavated by Ancient Egypt Research Associates immediately southeast of the pyramids was long called a "workers' town". AERA's own research has complicated that in a productive way: the site and its gallery complex probably belonged to a **major Nile port and harbour**, with basins, offloading quays, timber stockpiles, warehouses and possibly shipyards [24]. The galleries that read as barracks may have housed expeditionary crews who brought goods from the Levant in the north and Aswan in the south [24].

AERA's "Lost City" project describes the town as purpose-built by the crown to serve the construction of the pyramid complexes of Menkaure, Khafre and probably Khufu — housing not just labourers but the craftspeople, administrators, bakers, brewers and butchers who sustained them [25].

The implications run in both directions and are worth holding simultaneously: the site was simultaneously a workers' settlement and a functioning port, and "workers' town" and "port city" were not mutually exclusive [24]. Archaeological evidence for timber arriving from the Levant is a material confirmation of the Byblos Run trade route reaching Giza [26].

## The quarry roads

Where stone had to travel by land, it did so over engineered roads. The Old Kingdom basalt quarries at Widan el-Faras, 60 km southwest of Cairo in the northern Faiyum, connected to a quay on the shores of the former Lake Moeris at Qasr el-Sagha by an 11 km paved quarry road [27][28] — by the account of Bloxam, the oldest paved road in the world [16]. The road used basalt fragments alongside local sandstone, limestone and petrified wood [27].

This matters for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that the Egyptians of this period were capable of building and maintaining long, hard-surfaced haulage infrastructure — which is a precondition for the sledge haulage described in [Chapter 3](03-the-physics-of-hauling.md), because rollers are useless on sand and require a prepared road [29]. Second, the road's eastern terminus was a water transport point: the level of Lake Moeris stood about 20 m higher than today, allowing material to move by water to the Nile valley and then upstream to the pyramid fields [28].

## The other quarries

- **Tura-Masara**, east bank of the Nile about 12 km south of Cairo: 6 km of underground quarries in fine-grained white limestone; the source of casing for the 4th–6th Dynasty pyramids [11].
- **Gebel el-Silsila**, 64 km north of Aswan: sandstone, the standard temple material from the 18th Dynasty onward [27][16].
- **Gebel el-Asr / Chephren's Quarry**, Lower Nubia: the blue Chephren gneiss used for royal statuary and vessels [16].
- **Aswan**: granite and granodiorite, plus the dolerite quarried for pounders and toolstones [15][27].
- **Giza plateau itself**: local nummulitic limestone, the bulk of the core, plus the large quarry immediately south of the pyramid [1].

## Why the flood season set the schedule

Almost every part of the pyramid's construction was gated by the Nile's annual cycle. Boats could only carry loaded stone during the inundation [19]. Farming — and therefore the grain that fed the labour force and the beer that quenched it — followed the same cycle. So the construction year had two distinct regimes: a high-water shipping and quarrying season, and a low-water season for hauling, setting and finishing.

Merer's log covers Akhet (roughly June to November) [19][22]. Construction would have continued year-round at reduced intensity, but the *peak* throughput was structurally seasonal. This is why "how many workers" and "how many months" are not independent questions, and why workforce estimates that ignore seasonality tend to be inflated.

## An unresolved logistics problem: ramp debris

The Great Pyramid's ramps would themselves have been enormous — Arnold's estimate for a 1:10 straight ramp to the summit is about 8 million m³ of fill, more than three times the pyramid's own volume [30]. The material for such a ramp does not survive at Giza. Klemm and Klemm, and Müller-Römer, find too little ramp debris to account for large ramps [10]. Lehner reads the deposits of limestone chips, gypsum and *tafla* in the main quarry as dismantled ramps [10], and Hawass describes a ramp of the same materials southwest of the pyramid [10]. But Copas's careful formulation is the honest one: no direct physical evidence of a ramp system has been identified at Giza, and the usual explanation — that ramp material was dismantled and recycled — "has itself not been independently verified" [1].

Distinguishing an absence of evidence from evidence of absence is exactly the discipline this repository is trying to model. See [Chapter 10](10-evidence-grading-and-open-questions.md).
